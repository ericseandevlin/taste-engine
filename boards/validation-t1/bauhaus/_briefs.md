# Bauhaus / New Typography — validation briefs

Six content domains, each carrying the same visual program: off-center balance
built from weight rather than mirroring, one geometric sans family used
everywhere, color rationed to red/yellow/blue plus black on a pale ground,
hard-edged circles/squares/triangles/bars that push the layout around instead
of sitting in the corner as garnish, big jumps between the loudest and
quietest type on the page, deliberate open ground that the ink does not try
to fill, ragged-right body copy that starts flush left, thick printed-looking
bars carving the page into zones, at least one element knocked off the
horizontal, and — on at least one page — a word or letter blown up so large it
reads as a shape before it reads as language. Photography, where it appears,
stands in flat cut blocks rather than illustration.

Each brief below names the traps specific to that content type: the nearby
styles an underpowered extractor might confuse this for, and the failure
modes (a centered title stack, a filled-in page, a pastel palette, ornamental
type) that would sink the piece even if the palette looked roughly right.

---

## 1. Architecture-photography exhibition announcement

A city gallery's landing page announcing a photography survey of postwar
concrete civic buildings. Content: exhibition title, date range, venue
address, a pull-quote from the curator, a short list of featured
photographers, hours and admission.

Visual moves: the exhibition title runs enormous and off-axis, treated as a
poster headline rather than a page heading, sitting in the upper-left
two-thirds of the frame while the date/venue block anchors the lower right —
weight balanced diagonally, not mirrored. A thick black bar underlines the
title and continues past it to the page edge. A single flat red circle and a
flat yellow triangle sit as structural anchors near the photo grid, not as
decoration on it. The featured-photographer list sets flush left in the
geometric sans, ragged right, no justification. One photo block sits rotated
a few degrees off true, tilting against the rest of the orthogonal layout.

Traps to dodge: don't center the title over the venue info like a museum
gala invite — that symmetry is the wrong ancestry. Don't let the photo grid
tile edge-to-edge with no breathing room; the open paper has to carry visual
weight too. Keep the palette to the primary triad plus black and the page
ground; no sepia, no muted gallery-wall gray-greens. No serif for the venue
address, no matter how "elegant catalog" it might feel.

## 2. Letterpress print shop price list

A working price sheet for a small print shop: job categories (business
cards, letterheads, posters, wedding stationery), unit prices, turnaround
times, a note about paper stock options, contact info at the bottom.

Visual moves: the price table abandons a conventional grid-of-cells look —
row groups are separated by heavy horizontal rules of different weights, and
the price figures themselves jump dramatically in scale between the featured
service (largest job category, set huge) and the fine-print turnaround
notes. A black square and a red bar act as section dividers standing in for
where a ruled box would normally go. Column of category names sets flush
left in all-lowercase, single case throughout, no small caps or mixed-case
labels. A diagonal red rule cuts across one corner of the page pointing to
the "rush order" surcharge, giving that one fact a directional push.

Traps to dodge: this is the piece most likely to collapse into a boring
spreadsheet-in-a-browser or, at the other extreme, a cutesy "vintage
letterpress" pastiche with deckle edges, ink-texture overlays, or a
brown/cream antique palette — both are outside the style. No drop shadow
under the price box to fake a printed card. No centered column headers. Keep
the shape count purposeful: two or three primitives doing real structural
work, not a sprinkle of decorative circles scattered for texture, which
reads as Memphis rather than as load-bearing geometry.

## 3. University public lecture series page

A semester schedule of public lectures on city planning and design theory:
series title, six talks with speaker name, date, and one-line abstract, plus
a registration note.

Visual moves: each lecture entry is a module of its own — speaker name set
at a large scale, abstract line set small beneath it — and the six modules
are NOT arranged in a neat symmetrical grid; they stagger down the page at
slightly different left margins that still share a small set of common
alignment points, so the irregularity reads as considered rather than
sloppy. A blue square marks the currently-featured/next lecture, sized
larger than the black squares marking the rest. The series title sets in
lowercase at poster scale near the top, rotated slightly off horizontal,
functioning as the dominant shape on the page before it functions as a
sentence.

Traps to dodge: this is the brief most likely to drift into the Swiss/Int'l
Typographic Style neighbor by accident, because a lecture schedule wants a
tidy grid. Resist locking every module to the same column width in a strict
mathematical grid rendered in near-monochrome — keep at least the one blue
square and the rotated title as active color and diagonal moves so it does
not read as restrained Swiss systemization. Don't justify the abstract text
to force a clean right edge. Don't add a decorative rule or corner flourish
around each module — the separation must come from spacing and rule weight,
not ornament.

## 4. Community workshop enrollment page

A neighborhood workshop enrolling students for evening classes (bookbinding,
screenprinting, welding basics): course names, session dates, price, a
short enrollment call-to-action, and a supply list.

Visual moves: "ENROLL NOW" or similar sets as an oversized lowercase or
solid-caps block, the single loudest element on the page, pushed hard to one
side rather than centered as a banner. Course names run down the page in a
staggered flush-left list with big size differences between the class name
and its logistics line. A yellow bar and a black bar cross near the
enrollment block at right angles, one of them tilted diagonally rather than
purely horizontal/vertical, to point toward the call-to-action. Supply list
sets in a tight two-column arrangement that still rags right, never
justified.

Traps to dodge: don't let this slide into generic flat-design SaaS
sign-up-page territory — flat color blocks and rounded sans type alone are
not the style; the page needs genuine off-center tension, primitives that
carry structural weight, and a real 3x-or-bigger jump between the loudest
and quietest type, not the evenly-graded type scale a default UI kit would
use. No button with a soft shadow or gradient fill masquerading as a call to
action; the enroll block is a flat hard-edged shape. No decorative icon set
next to each course (no line-art scissors, no clip-art welding mask) —
photography or nothing.

## 5. Historical poster archive catalog page

A digital archive cataloging protest and concert posters from a defunct
print collective: archive title, a grid of catalog entries (poster title,
year, designer), a filter/search note, and a footer credit line.

Visual moves: the catalog grid itself is asymmetric — entries vary in
displayed size based on a "featured" flag rather than sitting in uniform
cells, so some entries take up much more page area than others, creating
the extreme hierarchy jump at the level of the whole grid rather than only
within text. Thick black rules separate the archive title zone from the
catalog body and again from the footer, functioning as structural
scaffolding rather than decorative underlines. One catalog entry's title
type is rotated and oversized to poster scale, standing in as the hero
image substitute where a real photograph might otherwise go. The palette
stays to red, blue, black, and the page ground; a small flat yellow tag
marks "featured" entries.

Traps to dodge: this is the piece most exposed to the Constructivist
misread, since poster archives lean into dense agitprop imagery by
association — pull back from packing the grid edge to edge with entries;
leave real shaped gaps between them. Avoid photomontage-heavy density in
favor of the more restrained flush-left, functional-catalog feel the brief
calls for. Don't let the filter/search UI look like a modern flat-design web
app with soft pill-shaped buttons — search controls should be flat
rectangles or bars, hard-edged, no rounded corners standing in as a "modern"
tell. No centered archive title banner across the top.

## 6. Design-collective manifesto excerpt page

A single long-form page presenting numbered excerpts from a small design
collective's manifesto on functional, honest-material design: a title
treatment, six to eight short numbered statements, and a closing line
naming the collective and year.

Visual moves: this is the page that leans hardest into letters as shapes —
the manifesto number for at least one statement is set at a scale far
larger than any surrounding text, functioning as a graphic anchor rather
than a list marker. Statements run flush left down the page at a staggered
set of left margins tied to a small number of shared guide positions, not a
single rigid column. A black bar the full width of one statement acts as a
structural divider mid-page, and a red square sits off to the side, rotated
to a diamond orientation, marking the collective's closing signature block.
The title itself sets in a single case, oversized, pushed into the upper
corner rather than centered over the page.

Traps to dodge: manifesto pages invite two wrong ancestries at once — Art
Deco's symmetric ornamental title treatment (avoid centering, avoid any
metallic or gradient "luxury" title effect, avoid stylized swash lettering)
and a filled-to-the-margins wall of numbered text that leaves no shaped
open ground (horror vacui). Keep hierarchy doing the work instead of
decorative rule frames or corner flourishes around the statement numbers.
Text stays ragged right throughout; do not justify the manifesto body to
make it look like a gray printed slab.
